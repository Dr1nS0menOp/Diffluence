# 09.P2: What people need

How to read this chapter: see [01-draft-and-published-spaces.md](01-draft-and-published-spaces.md).

Many teams keep text that a wide audience relies on: a product's user guide, an IT service catalogue, the procedures of a quality system, the policies of an organisation, a knowledge base for a help desk. The writers need room to draft, rework and discuss; the readers need a version that is finished, stable and consistent. Diffluence today puts both in one space: a page is published or not, and a page restriction keeps a draft from readers. That works for one page, but it fails at the scale of a space, for three reasons this chapter takes as its starting point.

1. **A draft is one setting away from readers.** In one space, what keeps a draft from readers is a restriction or a status on the page. One forgotten restriction, one move of a page out of a restricted branch, one restriction removed by someone who did not know why it was there, and the draft is in front of everyone. Plone documents the same trap: an item published inside a private folder is still found by search and opened by its address (sources, Plone). Drupal's staging documents that uploaded public files are reachable by address whatever the state of the content they belong to (sources, Drupal workspaces). The lesson is that a rule spread over many settings leaks; separation by construction does not.
2. **Readers need consistency across pages.** A guide of forty pages that changes for a new release must change together: half-updated, the old pages link to new ones that describe a feature the old ones do not know yet. Per-page publishing cannot give a reader a consistent whole.
3. **Readers and writers are different audiences.** The people who may read the finished guide (all staff, customers, guests) are usually many more than those who may see drafts (a team). In one space, every page needs both answers set right.

## 2.1 Who uses it

| Who | What they do | What they need |
|---|---|---|
| **Writer** | Drafts and changes pages in the draft space, alone or together | To write freely, without a reader seeing half-done work; to know what of theirs is not released yet |
| **Release manager** | Decides when the published space changes, often for a product release or a policy date | To see what is out of step, to release one page, a branch or everything, to leave pages out, and to take pages down |
| **Reviewer or approver** | Approves pages under document control (spec 064) | That approving is enough to release, without a second step |
| **Reader** | Reads the published space | A consistent, finished space; no sign of drafts; links that work |
| **Space administrator** | Sets up both spaces and who can do what in each | To link two spaces safely, and to know a draft cannot reach readers by a page setting |
| **Auditor** | Checks what readers saw, when, and who released it | A record per release, with the versions on both sides |

## 2.2 What other tools and research teach

Read on 2026-11-14 for behaviour only, from public documentation; see [sources.md](sources.md). Each tool is described in our own words.

- **A publishing app for a commercial wiki** (the behaviour blueprint 08 names). Two spaces in one site are linked, a draft source and a published target, which must start empty. Each page carries a state such as new, out of sync or published; a page edited in the source becomes out of sync until it or the whole space is published again. The published side keeps its own version numbers and starts at version 1. Publishing copies the title, the body, attachments and ordinary labels; it does not copy comments, page permissions, the page status, personal marks or the version history. A space publish sends only new and out-of-sync pages after the first time. Links between pages of the source are pointed at the matching page of the target; links to other spaces stay as they are. A page deleted in the source stays in the target with a mark, and a page whose parent is missing goes to the top of the target. With a workflow app, a page can publish when it reaches the workflow's final state, and a space publish can be limited to pages in that state. The home page and blog posts are not published, only one target per source is allowed, and only one space publish runs at a time. *What we take:* the pair, the per-page state, what is and is not copied, links rewritten inside the pair, approval that publishes. *What we do differently:* the published space is managed, so a hand edit there cannot drift (PUB-1403); deletes and archives can be mirrored (PUB-1410); a page goes under its nearest released ancestor, not to the top (PUB-1426); the home page is released (PUB-1418); restrictions in the draft keep a page out of a bulk release (PUB-1405); a run releases exactly what its preview showed (PUB-1409).
- **A CMS with staging workspaces.** Changes are made in a workspace layered over the live site and published to live as one unit; content changed in one workspace is locked for other workspaces until it is published or discarded, because structured content cannot be merged safely; deleting in a workspace hides the item there while live keeps it until publish; files stored as public are reachable by address regardless of their state. *What we take:* release as one unit (PUB-1411); no merging of two sources (PUB-1402); files must not be shared across the boundary (PUB-1419).
- **A CMS with content moderation.** A published revision stays live while a newer draft revision waits beside it; publishing the draft makes it the default revision and the old one is kept. *What we take:* this is document control's model (DOC-300) and is what pages under a workflow in the draft space already do.
- **An enterprise CMS with separate author and publish tiers.** Authors work on one system and activation copies a page to the publish system. A quick publish copies one page and the items it references that are not live yet; a managed publication lets the person see and untick the referenced items, include child pages, and schedule; unpublishing a page does not unpublish what it references. *What we take:* a preview that lists what a release will also bring along (PUB-430), and taking off as a separate act (PUB-440).
- **A headless CMS with releases.** Changes to many documents are grouped in a release, previewed, and published together, on demand or at a time. *What we take:* release of a whole set at once, and the preview of what readers will get; scheduling is later (PUB-1510).
- **A CMS with draft and publish per item.** Each entry has a draft and a published version; the API returns the draft unless asked for the published one. *What we take:* the opposite default: every reader's path gets the published copy, because it is a different space.
- **An open-source wiki with a publication workflow.** A draft page and a published page are two documents; the published one only changes by publishing from the draft; rights on drafts are handled by the workflow and rights on published pages by the administrator; unpublishing removes the published copy. An open issue asks for links between nested pages to be rewritten. *What we take:* the published copy is changed only by publishing (PUB-1403); link rewriting is a requirement from the first wave, not a later fix (06).
- **A wiki engine with flagged revisions.** Readers see the latest reviewed ("stable") version while newer edits wait for review; lists show pages with pending changes and pages never reviewed. A study of seventeen language editions found that this kind of prepublication review was very effective at keeping low-quality edits from readers, had at most moderate effects on the amount and quality of contributions, and gave some sign of discouraging newcomers; a later study found that support for it waned over time, partly because of the review backlog. *What we take:* the protection is real, and the cost is the backlog; so the release screen is built around the backlog (what is out of step, oldest first, PUB-1220), and writers are never blocked from writing (PUB-104).
- **Docs as code.** Documentation lives in a repository; every change is built into a preview that reviewers open; production is deployed after the change is merged, often by hand, sometimes through a staging copy that matches production. *What we take:* a reviewer must see the result as readers will (PUB-432, compare with the released version), and the step to production is a deliberate act.

## 2.3 Requirements

- **PUB-100** · new · Writers work in a **draft space** that readers of the published space cannot open, and readers work in a **published space** that only a release can change. *Why:* separation by construction is what makes "a draft cannot leak through a page setting" true (reason 1 above); ISO 9001 7.5.3.1 asks that documented information is protected and available where needed, and 7.5.3.2 asks for control of distribution and access. *Edge cases:* a person may belong to both audiences; they see both spaces, each as what it is (PUB-603). *Proof:* the leak matrix of chapter 07.
- **PUB-101** · new · Every page of the draft space shows its **release status** in words, and the space has one list of what is out of step (chapter 04). *Why:* a release manager cannot release what they cannot find; flagged revisions show that a backlog no one sees grows. *Proof:* tests per status.
- **PUB-102** · new · A release can be of **one page**, a **branch**, or the **whole space** (chapter 05). *Why:* a typo fix should not wait for a product release, and a product release must change everything at once. *Proof:* tests per scope.
- **PUB-103** · new · Pages can be **left out** of releases, and pages can be **taken off** the published space, as separate acts (PUB-420, PUB-440). *Why:* working notes, half-finished sections and retired pages exist in every draft space. *Proof:* tests.
- **PUB-104** · new · Writing in the draft space is never blocked by releasing: a run reads immutable versions, so people keep writing while it runs (PUB-1409). *Why:* the cost of prepublication review in research was the burden on contributors; Diffluence must not add a lock to writing. *Proof:* a test that publishing in the draft during a run succeeds and leaves the run unchanged.
- **PUB-105** · new · **Links** between pages of the draft space lead to the published copies in the published space (chapter 06). *Why:* a published space whose links lead back into the draft space would be broken for readers. *Proof:* chapter 06.
- **PUB-106** · new · Under document control, **approval releases** (chapter 08). *Why:* the roadmap's "can follow a workflow, so approval publishes"; regulated teams want one act, the approval, to be what changes what readers see. *Proof:* chapter 08.
- **PUB-107** · new · Every release, take-off and setting change is **audited**, with both sides' versions (chapter 10). *Why:* ISO 9001 7.5.3.2 asks for control of changes and retention; an auditor asks what readers saw on a date. *Proof:* chapter 10.
- **PUB-108** · new · A reader of the published space sees **no sign** of the draft space: not its name, not its pages, not who wrote what, not that a newer version waits. *Why:* the existence of a draft can itself be confidential, for example a reorganisation or a product not announced. *Edge cases:* people who may view the draft see the link between the two (PUB-1230). *Proof:* tests that render the published page, its history, search, the feed, exports and notifications for a reader and look for the draft space's name and ids.
- **PUB-109** · new · A space administrator can set up a pair, change its settings and end it, each with a **preview** of what changes for readers (chapter 03). *Why:* turning a space into a published space changes who may edit it; nobody should learn that afterwards. *Proof:* tests of each preview.

## 2.4 What people asked for that this study does not do

Listed with reasons in chapter 15: two drafts into one, several audiences from one draft, releasing between installations, merging edits made in the published space back into the draft, and releasing part of a page.

## 2.5 Scenarios used through this study

- **S1, a user guide.** A team of six writes the guide of a product in a draft space; the whole company and customers read the published space. At each product release, the release manager releases the whole space; between releases, single pages are released for corrections.
- **S2, a quality system.** A space of procedures under a space workflow (spec 064), with signatures; approval of a procedure releases it into the published space that all staff read. A withdrawn procedure leaves the published space at once.
- **S3, a help-desk knowledge base.** A large space of 3,000 pages, changed by twenty people daily, released every evening by a release manager, with a branch of internal notes left out.
- **S4, an existing space turned into a pair.** A team with a populated space starts a draft space from it, and from then on edits only in the draft.
